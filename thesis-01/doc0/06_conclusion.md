# Chapter 6 — Conclusion

## 6.1 Summary of the Work

This thesis presented HoneyIQ, a simulation framework for adaptive honeypot management in which a structured, intent-driven attacker — modelled on the Lockheed Martin Cyber Kill Chain (Hutchins et al., 2011) — is opposed by two defender policies: the Stage-Escalation Decision Matrix (SEDM) and a Deep Q-Network (DQN) baseline. This version extends that framework in three directions — more realistic synthetic traffic, a severity-weighted alternative escalation signal, and two auditable mechanisms for dynamic behavioural adjustment — and uses the project's own DQN results as direct evidence when deciding how that third extension should be designed.

The work was motivated by four observations, extending the three that motivated the original system. First, contemporary cyber adversaries conduct structured, multi-stage campaigns that progress predictably through reconnaissance, weaponization, delivery, exploitation, installation, command-and-control, and actions on objectives. Second, honeypots provide a uniquely valuable defensive layer that can detect, observe, and deceive attackers without disrupting production systems, but their effectiveness depends on the quality of the response policy. Third, learned policies based on deep reinforcement learning — while capable of high performance — are fundamentally opaque and therefore difficult to deploy in operational security environments that require auditability and explainability. Fourth, a policy fixed entirely at design time cannot account for information that only becomes available at runtime — a source's attack history, or whether a given rule is firing so often it has stopped being informative — and addressing this by reaching directly for a learned mechanism risks reintroducing the opacity problem the third observation already identifies.

To address these challenges, this thesis made the following contributions.

### HoneyIQ Simulation Framework

A fully self-contained, Gymnasium-compatible simulation environment implementing:

- A 24-dimensional state space combining one-hot attack type, kill chain stage, and attacker intent with continuous threat level, attack count, and escalation rate.
- A five-action honeypot policy interface (ALLOW, LOG, TROLL, BLOCK, ALERT) mapping response severity from passive passthrough to immediate escalation.
- A domain-informed reward function with three layers: a 5×5 base matrix, a late-stage amplifier for kill chain stages 4–6, and attack-type-specific bonuses encoding honeypot best practices.
- A comprehensive metrics layer computing per-step and per-episode statistics including detection rate, false-positive rate, and composite risk, with visualisations spanning both the original evaluation and the new extensions.

All experiments are fully reproducible without access to live network data.

### Intent-Aware Markov Attacker with Session-Coherent Traffic Realism

A coupled pair of Discrete-Time Markov Chains governing attack-type and kill-chain-stage transitions, with four intent profiles encoded as element-wise transition modifiers:

- **Stealthy**: slow, low-severity campaigns biased toward reconnaissance and backdoor activity.
- **Aggressive**: fast, high-severity campaigns biased toward DoS, Worms, and Exploits.
- **Targeted**: focused exploitation chains progressing from Exploits through Shellcode to Backdoors.
- **Opportunistic**: scattered campaigns with moderate escalation speed and diverse attack types.

Network features are sampled from per-attack parametric distributions calibrated to UNSW-NB15 signatures, providing a bridge between the simulation and real-world traffic characteristics. The kill-chain floor constraint ensures temporal consistency by preventing unrealistic stage regression. New in this version, each simulated session draws a persistent intensity multiplier and, for benign traffic, one of three behavioural personas, so that a session's traffic is no longer an independent draw at every step but reads as one coherent source — and benign traffic, previously a single distribution, now spans a small but qualitatively distinct population of sources.

### Stage-Escalation Decision Matrix (SEDM) with a Fourth Override Rule

The principal technical contribution: an interpretable, deterministic, zero-training-overhead policy implementing a five-step decision algorithm:

1. Compute escalation risk analytically from the intent-specific Markov transition matrix.
2. Classify the risk into Low, Medium, or High band.
3. Perform a 7×3 matrix lookup.
4. Apply four prioritised override rules (R4: cross-session reputation → upgrade, checked first; R1: Normal → ALLOW; R2: DOS/WORMS → upgrade; R3: high rate → upgrade).
5. Log a composite risk score for situational awareness.

The SEDM's transparency is its defining property: every decision is traceable to a specific matrix entry and override condition, enabling full audit without post-hoc explanation approximations — a property this version preserves exactly while adding genuine runtime adaptivity through R4.

### Composite Threat-Level Metric and Severity-Weighted Escalation Tracking

A weighted combination (attack severity 45%, kill chain stage 35%, escalation rate 15%, cumulative count 5%) providing a normalised threat index that drives both the reward function and the SEDM's composite risk score. The weight assignment reflects domain reasoning about the relative informational value of each signal. New in this version, the escalation-rate component of this metric can be computed either by the original hard sliding window or by a severity-weighted exponential moving average, computed side by side so the two can be compared directly (§4.10) rather than substituted blindly.

### Auditable Dynamic Response

Two new mechanisms allow the SEDM's behaviour to respond to runtime observation while remaining fully traceable by construction: a `ReputationTracker` maintaining a half-life-decayed offense score per source address, independent of individual session lifetimes, feeding the new R4 override; and a bounded deadband controller (`AdaptiveThresholds`) that keeps the R3 override's trigger frequency near an operator-specified target, explicitly scoped as an alert-fatigue safety valve rather than a correctness mechanism. Both default to reproducing the original system's exact prior behaviour when not explicitly engaged.

### DQN Baseline, Comparison, and a Direct Answer on Dynamic-Response Design

A full DQN implementation (24 → 256 → 128 → 64 → 5 with LayerNorm, experience replay, target network, Huber loss, and epsilon-greedy exploration) trained for 300 episodes on the Opportunistic intent. The DQN achieves comparable detection rates (>98.5%) to the SEDM but requires 150,000 training steps, produces an opaque policy, and exhibits undefined false-positive rate characteristics due to the dense-attack training distribution. This version uses that specific, already-collected evidence — together with this project's own documented evaluation-harness defect history — as the basis for a direct, project-grounded argument (§5.3) that the auditable mechanisms above, not a learned mechanism, were the appropriate choice for dynamic response at this stage of the project.

## 6.2 Key Findings

**Finding 1: Analytical escalation risk enables exact, training-free adaptation.** The Markov chain structure provides exact, per-intent escalation probabilities that the SEDM uses directly. This avoids the data dependency of RL-based approaches while correctly capturing the intent-specific differences in campaign tempo. The result is a policy that adapts to four qualitatively different attack profiles without any training or parameter tuning.

**Finding 2: Detection rates above 99% are achievable with a deterministic matrix.** The SEDM achieves detection rates of 99.93–100.0% across all four attacker intents under classifier-driven decisions. This performance is competitive with the DQN baseline and substantially exceeds what a random policy would achieve. The result challenges the common assumption that high detection rate requires a learned, adaptive policy.

**Finding 3: The near-zero false positive rate is a measured property of the current synthetic setup, not a general immunity claim — and this claim is now supported by two independent lines of evidence rather than one.** The measured false positive rate is 0.00% across all four intents under classifier-driven decisions, following from the R1 override's unconditional NORMAL→ALLOW mapping combined with the classifier's near-perfect (99.85%) separation of the synthetic attack-type distributions. A feature-noise robustness sweep shows the false-positive rate rises predictably and monotonically as the classifier's input signal is made more realistic via artificial noise injection (up to 10.00% at 50% injected feature noise). This version adds a second, independent probe that reaches the same conclusion without any artificial noise: making the traffic generator itself more realistic (session-coherent intensity, multi-persona benign traffic, §3.2.4) drops classifier accuracy from 99.85% to 99.40% and NORMAL-class recall from 1.0000 to 0.9900 (§4.9), through the same underlying mechanism (reduced class separability) as the noise sweep. Two structurally different interventions degrading the same headline number in the same direction, for the same underlying reason, is stronger evidence than either alone that the 0.00% figure was never a general immunity claim.

**Finding 4: Interpretability and detection performance are compatible — including for the dynamic-adjustment capability RL is usually reached for.** The SEDM achieves detection rates comparable to the DQN with full interpretability, zero training overhead, and superior cross-intent consistency. This finding supports the recommendation of Rudin (2019) to prefer interpretable models in high-stakes domains when the problem structure permits a good interpretable solution — and the kill chain model clearly provides such a structure. This version extends the finding to a capability usually assumed to require learning: runtime behavioural adjustment. The reputation override and the bounded threshold controller (§3.10) demonstrate that genuine, observation-driven dynamic response is achievable within the same fully-auditable design family as the original SEDM, without a training loop or an opaque function approximator.

**Finding 5: DQN specialises to the training distribution.** The DQN's effective strategy of "almost never ALLOW" achieves high detection on the Opportunistic training distribution but would not generalise to scenarios with more balanced benign/attack traffic. This specialisation is an artefact of the dense-attack training environment, not an inherent limitation of DQN, but it illustrates the importance of training distribution design for RL-based security policies — and, in this version, serves as the direct evidence justifying the decision not to build dynamic response as a learned mechanism (§5.3).

**Finding 6: A severity-weighted escalation signal produces a materially different, and arguably more design-intent-consistent, override-trigger regime than the original frequency-based signal.** Under the continuous-attack evaluation protocol used throughout this thesis, the original window-based escalation signal saturates near its maximum for most of a typical episode, causing the R3 override to fire on 44.7–90.0% of decisions across the four intents — closer to a background condition than the "sustained, dense attack traffic" trigger the threshold was designed to detect. The severity-weighted EMA alternative introduced in this version fires far more selectively (0.7–22.0%) because its steady-state ceiling is bounded by attack severity rather than by 1.0 (§4.10). Both behaviours are internally consistent with their respective signal definitions; the finding is that the choice of escalation signal has large, previously unmeasured, operational consequences.

**Finding 7: Cross-session reputation closes a specific, previously-identified evasion gap, deterministically and after a small, known number of offenses.** A repeat offender's superficially benign opening event, on returning after session expiry, is no longer treated as first contact once four EXPLOITS-severity prior offenses have accumulated (fewer for higher-severity attack types, more for lower-severity ones) — a threshold-crossing behaviour confirmed identically across 30 independently simulated source addresses (§4.11). This directly operationalises the "camouflaging attacker" concern that an earlier draft of this thesis's evaluation claimed empirical support for and later withdrew as a measurement artefact (§4.7); R4 is a structural answer to the underlying concern, independent of that earlier withdrawn claim.

**Finding 8: A narrowly-scoped, honestly-framed adaptive mechanism can be evaluated honestly too — including reporting where it does not help much.** The bounded `RATE_THRESHOLD` controller reduces R3's trigger rate substantially under mixed-traffic conditions (7.60% → 4.40%) but only marginally under the fully-saturated continuous-attack conditions used in most of this thesis's primary evaluation (45.70% → 44.40%), because its bounded design cannot compensate for an escalation signal saturated for reasons unrelated to the controller (§4.12). This is reported as a genuine finding about the mechanism's operating limits, not smoothed over — a direct consequence of scoping the mechanism's claims narrowly and honestly in the first place (§3.10.2, §5.3).

## 6.3 Implications for Practice

The results of this thesis suggest several practical implications for honeypot management system design:

1. **Start with an interpretable policy.** The SEDM's performance, immediate operability, and auditability make it the appropriate default for initial deployment. A security team can review, understand, and modify the 7-row matrix without requiring machine learning expertise — and, as of this version, can extend it with genuine runtime adaptivity (reputation tracking, bounded threshold control) without giving up that property.
2. **Use the kill chain model explicitly.** The strong correlation between kill chain stage and appropriate response severity (ALLOW at Reconnaissance, ALERT at C2 and beyond) is a robust design principle that should be reflected in any honeypot policy, whether interpretable or learned.
3. **Calibrate expectations on false positives by input signal quality, not attacker profile.** Under the current evaluation, false positive rate does not vary meaningfully by attacker intent (0.00% for all four); it is driven instead by classifier input fidelity, and — as this version's traffic-realism results confirm — by how realistically that fidelity is itself modelled. Deployments should budget for false positives proportional to the expected noise and diversity in their feature-extraction pipeline and traffic population, not assume a single intent profile is inherently noisier than another.
4. **Treat cross-session memory as a first-class design requirement, not an afterthought.** A policy evaluated only within single, independent sessions cannot represent — and therefore cannot be credited or debited for — its behaviour toward a source that returns. This version's reputation mechanism shows that adding this capability does not require abandoning interpretability, and that its behaviour (a specific, auditable offense-count threshold) can be characterised precisely rather than left as a qualitative claim.
5. **Adopt bounded, narrowly-scoped adaptive mechanisms before learned ones, and evaluate them against their actual, stated purpose.** The threshold controller in this version demonstrates both halves of this recommendation: it was scoped honestly (an alert-fatigue safety valve, not a correctness mechanism) precisely because no signal existed to justify a stronger claim, and it was evaluated against that stated purpose rather than a more impressive-sounding one it was never designed to meet — surfacing a genuine regime-dependent limitation (§4.12) that a less honestly-scoped evaluation might have obscured.
6. **Use RL for refinement only after a real feedback pathway exists, not as the default path to "adaptive."** A DQN trained alongside a deployed SEDM — using live honeypot interactions and, critically, real analyst-confirmed labels rather than a proxy signal — could discover refinements to the matrix or its thresholds that are not immediately obvious to human designers. This version's contribution is to make the *gate* on that refinement stage concrete: build the analyst-labelling pathway first (§5.6), rather than treating "collect more training data" as a sufficient condition on its own.

## 6.4 Closing Remarks

HoneyIQ demonstrates that the mathematical structure of the Markov attacker model — specifically, the analytically computable escalation probabilities derived from intent-specific transition matrices — provides sufficient information to design a high-quality, interpretable honeypot policy without any machine learning. The Stage-Escalation Decision Matrix translates the probabilistic dynamics of the kill chain into a human-readable response protocol that aligns with honeypot best practices: engaging low-severity traffic passively, tarpitting persistent attackers for intelligence, and escalating decisively as campaigns reach critical kill-chain stages.

This version extends that demonstration to a question the original system left open: whether *dynamic* behaviour — responding to a source's history, or to the system's own operating characteristics — necessarily requires abandoning that interpretability. It does not. A half-life-decayed reputation score and a bounded deadband controller, each reducible at any instant to a handful of named numbers a human analyst can verify directly, provide genuine runtime adaptivity without a training loop, a reward function that can silently teach the wrong lesson, or a model that requires after-the-fact explanation to the people who built it. This project's own DQN baseline — its documented convergence to a distributional shortcut rather than genuine discrimination, and the independently-recurring label-alignment defects in the evaluation code that would have fed a reward signal built the same way — is not cited here as a generic objection to reinforcement learning, but as concrete, project-specific evidence that this particular shortcut was not yet available to take safely, and a precise statement (§5.6) of what would need to be true before it was.

The simulation infrastructure provides a principled, self-contained foundation for future research. Replacing synthetic feature distributions with real data fits, re-evaluating the already-integrated classifier-driven decision pipeline under realistic feature-measurement noise, modelling an adaptive attacker who responds to the defender's actions, extending to a multi-node network topology, and — new in this version — building the analyst-labelling pathway that would responsibly enable a future learning-based refinement stage, are natural next steps that would progressively close the gap between simulation and operational deployment.

As networked systems face increasingly sophisticated adversaries, the combination of structured attacker models, analytically-grounded decision policies, auditable runtime adaptivity, and rigorous, honestly-scoped simulation-based evaluation offers a principled path toward honeypot management that is simultaneously effective, trustworthy, adaptive, and deployable in the operational environments that need it most.
